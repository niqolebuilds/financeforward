Bridge 3 — You belong here

Subject You belong here

The quiet voice says you'd be out of your depth. It's wrong — and here's why.

Let me guess what's stopping you: a quiet voice saying "a hackathon is for the tech people; I'd be out of my depth." I want to answer it directly and kindly, because it's wrong, and it's the only thing between you and something you'd genuinely enjoy. You belong at this hackathon, exactly as you are — and I can back that up.

The biggest myth first: you do not need to know how to code. No-code tools do the heavy lifting — you describe what you want in plain language and they build it. The valuable person in the room isn't the one who writes software; it's the one who deeply understands the problem worth solving. That's you: you know which denial is painful, which report is a slog, which question gets asked over and over. That knowledge is the rare ingredient. And the second myth — that you might "do it wrong"? There are no wrong answers in a hackathon. The point is to learn by doing. A prototype that half-works taught you something; everyone in the room is a beginner, including the confident-looking ones.

What the day feels like, and how you're judged. The day has a friendly, well-supported rhythm: a kickoff to form teams and share ideas, a working session where you build with mentors and starter kits nearby, a show-and-tell where each team demos, and light judging (Figure B3.1). It's a lively workshop, not a high-pressure exam. And the judging — the Three I's of Idea, Implementation, and Impact — actually favors you: two of the three depend on understanding the problem and its value, which is your home turf, not on engineering. There are documented cases of internal teams shipping multiple production-ready tools in a couple of days, and the people who make that happen are domain experts, not just coders.

When it's your turn to present, one simple story always works (Figure B3.2): show the pain (the annoying task today), show it working (your tool, live), show one number (time saved or estimated impact). Pain, proof, payoff. You don't need a polished pitch — you need the room to feel the problem and see that your thing helps. It maps right onto the Three I's, and anyone can tell it, especially about a task they know intimately. That's the whole performance.

Hold onto the throughlines one last time, because they make this safe to say yes to. Nothing you build removes the human — a chatbot answers, an agent acts, and in both cases a person keeps the last word. Fear and excitement can sit together; the invitation isn't to be fearless, it's to be curious anyway. You've spent fifteen issues learning why this matters and how the guardrails work. The hackathon is simply where you get to put your hands on it, safely, among friends who are all figuring it out together.

So here's my warm, low-stakes invitation (Figure B3.3). The Siloam AI hackathon is this December. Don't over-prepare — just bring the annoying task you've been carrying and an open mind. Jot down one idea this week, and seriously consider signing up. Come be a beginner alongside the rest of us and find out how much you can build in a day. The room is genuinely better with you in it, because you know the problems that matter most. You belong there. I really hope you'll come — and I can't wait to see what you make.
