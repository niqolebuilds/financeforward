Bridge 1 — Your issues are ideas

Subject Your issues are ideas

Why the task you dread most is usually the best hackathon idea you have — and the simple filter that tells you when AI can help.

Rahmi dreads one task above all: every week she works the same pile of low-dollar denials — reading each, checking the payer rule, drafting the hundredth version of the same appeal. Not hard, just soul-wearingly repetitive. For years she filed it under "part of the job." Then someone said one sentence that reframed it: "That's not just an annoying task — that's an idea. That's something you could build a tool to help with." She'd never thought of her own frustration as raw material. But that's exactly what it is.

That's the mindset shift this whole bridge rests on: your daily issues are ideas in disguise (Figure B1.1). Every denial you dread, every reconciliation you chase, every payer question you answer for the fifth time is a buildable opportunity wearing the costume of a chore. The move is from problem-spotter to solution-seeker — from enduring the friction to asking, "what if something could help with this?" And the tasks you find most tedious are usually the ones most worth automating, understood best by the person who does them.

A filter, and a mental model. Here's how to know when the answer is probably yes. If a task mostly involves reading, writing, or deciding, AI can likely help (Figure B1.2). Reading a claim or policy is reading; drafting an appeal is writing; routing, matching, or prioritizing is deciding. Rahmi's denial work is all three — which is why it's such a strong candidate. And to keep the tools from feeling like magic, hold one model: AI = Understand + Reason + Act. It understands the information, reasons about what fits, and acts by drafting and queuing — tirelessly, on the boring parts, with you still making every real decision.

The economics now back the invitation. The cost of AI inference fell roughly 280x in about 18 months, and 78% of organizations already use AI in at least one function. Building has gotten cheap enough that a non-engineer can turn an idea into a working prototype in a day. So the scarce resource was never technical skill or budget — it's the shift from seeing an annoying task as "just the job" to seeing it as buildable. You are the expert on your own friction, which makes you the ideal person to spot the idea inside it.

Keep the throughline in view, because it's what makes this safe to get excited about. Nothing here removes the human. The tool Rahmi imagines still hands her the drafts to review; she still decides which appeals go out; she's still accountable. A chatbot would answer her question about a denial — an agent would help her work the denials — and in both cases she keeps the last word. Excitement and a little caution can ride together here; the invitation is to build, with the guardrails we've spent the whole series learning.

Rahmi's dreaded denials are now the idea she's bringing to the hackathon (Figure B1.3). She hasn't built anything yet and she's not an engineer — but she has the only prerequisite that matters: she knows the problem cold and has stopped seeing it as merely a chore. You already have your ideas, too. You've been complaining about them for years. This December, we turn the complaints into builds.
