Bridge 2 — Think like a builder

Subject Think like a builder

There are four ways to build, from very simple to more involved — and you almost always start with the simplest.

People new to building imagine the most complicated version — a sophisticated system wired into every database (Figure B2.1) — get overwhelmed, and opt out. Let's do the opposite. There are four ways to build, from very simple to more involved, and you almost always start with the simplest. Thinking like a builder is mostly about picking the right-sized tool and scoping it small enough to finish.

The four patterns, in plain finance language and in order of effort: Pattern 1 — a simple AI feature (prompt in, useful output: summarize this denial, draft this reply). Pattern 2 — RAG (answers grounded in our own policies and contracts, with citations). Pattern 3 — agents (a goal drives multi-step, tool-using work, with you reviewing). Pattern 4 — MCP (the universal connector — "USB-C for AI" — that lets agents reach our systems safely). The rule: start at Pattern 1 and layer up only if the problem truly demands it. Most great hackathon projects are Pattern 1 done well.

Which problems, and how small. To choose what's worth building, use the AI Opportunity Framework — strong candidates involve lots of text or conversation, repetitive decisions, manual research, or slow processes (Figure B2.2). Finance and RCM overflow with all four. Then scope ruthlessly: aim for something SMALL (one workflow, not your whole job), REAL (a problem you actually have), VISIBLE (pain people feel), and DEMO-ABLE (you can show it work live). A project that fits all four is finishable in a hackathon and beats an ambitious idea you never get running.

There's a reason to insist on starting low on the ladder, and it's the most common failure mode: pattern-chasing. Teams reach for agents or MCP because they sound advanced, when a Pattern 1 feature would have solved it in a fraction of the time. Treat sophistication as a cost, not a badge — every rung up buys capability at the price of effort and failure risk. Start simple, prove value, climb only on evidence. That sequencing is the single biggest predictor of whether an idea becomes a working demo by show-and-tell.

Keep the guardrail in the frame as you design. Whichever pattern you pick, the human stays in the loop — a Pattern 1 tool hands you a draft to check; an agent queues its work for your approval; nothing touches a real account without a person. Scoping small isn't only about finishing on time; it's also what keeps a first build safe and easy to govern. Small, real, and reviewable is the sweet spot where excitement and caution shake hands.

So thinking like a builder isn't technical (Figure B2.3). It's two questions asked of your annoying task: what's the simplest pattern that could help (usually Pattern 1), and how do I scope this small enough to demo it? Start simple, stay small, layer up only if needed. You already have the judgment for right-sizing — it's the same instinct you use on every other problem in your work.
