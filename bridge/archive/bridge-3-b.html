Bridge 3 — You belong here

Subject Why internal hackathons are a capability strategy, not a morale event

In one documented case, a team shipped three production-ready AI solutions in 48 hours.

Behind the friendly invitation is a serious capability strategy. Internal hackathons are one of the most effective ways to build AI capability, and the results can be striking — in one documented case, a team produced three production-ready AI solutions in 48 hours. A hackathon isn't a morale event that happens to touch AI; it's a compressed capability-building program with a working-software output. That's why we're running one.

The day has a proven structure worth knowing before you commit (Figure B3.1): kickoff (form teams, share ideas) → working session (build, with mentors and starter kits) → show-and-tell (demo) → judging. This format is deliberately low-friction and high-support: starter kits reduce setup time, mentors unblock non-technical builders, and the short cycle forces the small scoping that produces finishable projects. The structure is what makes it accessible to people who've never built anything.

Judging is transparent and, notably, not about technical polish. Projects are evaluated on the Three I's: Idea (is the problem worth solving?), Implementation (does the prototype work?), and Impact (what value would it create?). Two of the three — Idea and Impact — are exactly where a finance/RCM expert has the advantage, because they depend on understanding the problem and its value, not on engineering. A domain expert who scoped well and can articulate impact competes strongly regardless of coding ability.

The demo that wins follows a simple, learnable arc (Figure B3.2): show the pain (the task today), show it working (the prototype live), show one metric (time saved or estimated impact). This structure de-risks the presentation — you're not selling vision, you're demonstrating a concrete before/after with a number. It's a format anyone can execute, and it maps directly onto the Three I's: pain establishes the Idea, the live demo is Implementation, and the metric is Impact.

There's a continuity dividend that matters for ROI. The value of a hackathon isn't only the prototypes built in the room; it's the capability and belief that persist, plus the pipeline of ideas that can become real products with follow-through. Organizations that plan for continuity — a path from prototype to production — convert a two-day event into lasting operational improvement. That's the difference between a fun offsite and a genuine capability program, and it's how a single December can seed a year of builds.

The bottom line for anyone hesitating on the sidelines (Figure B3.3): the economics and the scoring both favor participation by domain experts, not just engineers. The scarce input is deep problem knowledge, the format supplies the technical support, and the judging rewards exactly what you bring. Sitting out isn't the safe choice — it's leaving the highest-leverage capability-building opportunity of the year on the table.

Forward this to your team as the pitch: internal hackathons build real capability, the judging rewards domain expertise, and the December event is our highest-leverage chance to start.
