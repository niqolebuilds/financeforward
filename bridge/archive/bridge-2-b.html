Bridge 2 — Think like a builder

Subject The scoping discipline behind finishable hackathon projects

Behind good hackathon projects is a boring truth: they're scoped well, not built cleverly.

Behind good hackathon projects is a boring truth: they're scoped well, not built cleverly. Thinking like a builder is a decision discipline, and it starts with knowing your four options in order of effort (Figure B2.1). Pattern 1 — a simple AI feature (prompt in, useful output). Pattern 2 — RAG (answers grounded in our own documents, with citations). Pattern 3 — agents (a goal drives a multi-step, tool-using workflow). Pattern 4 — MCP (a universal connector — "USB-C for AI" — that lets agents reach our systems safely). The rule is simple: start at Pattern 1 and layer up only when the problem demands it.

Each pattern has a cost-and-capability profile. Pattern 1 is near-zero effort and solves a surprising share of real problems — it should be your default hypothesis. Pattern 2 (RAG) adds modest effort to gain grounding and citations, ideal where answers must trace to our policies. Pattern 3 (agents) adds orchestration to handle multi-step workflows. Pattern 4 (MCP) is the integration layer for connecting to live systems. Effort and risk rise as you climb, so the ROI-maximizing move is the lowest pattern that solves the problem, not the most impressive one.

To pick which problems are worth building for at all, apply the AI Opportunity Framework (Figure B2.2). Strong candidates share four traits: lots of text or conversation, repetitive decisions, manual research, or slow processes. These are the signatures of tasks where AI creates value, and finance/RCM work is saturated with them — claims and policies (text), routing and matching (repetitive decisions), status and eligibility checks (manual research), and multi-day closes (slow processes). Score your backlog against these four and the winners self-identify.

Then scope ruthlessly. A hackathon-viable project is SMALL (one workflow), REAL (a genuine problem), VISIBLE (felt pain), and DEMO-ABLE (shows working live). This isn't just event advice; it's risk management. Small, real, visible, demo-able projects have a high completion probability and a clear success signal, which is exactly what turns a prototype into momentum. Over-scoped projects fail silently and demoralize; well-scoped ones ship and inspire the next build.

The framework also protects against the most common failure mode: pattern-chasing (Figure B2.3). Teams often reach for agents or MCP because they sound advanced, when a Pattern 1 feature would have solved the problem in a fraction of the time. The disciplined builder treats sophistication as a cost, not a badge — every rung up the ladder buys capability at the price of effort and failure risk. Start low, prove value, climb only on evidence. That sequencing is the single biggest predictor of whether a hackathon idea becomes a working demo.

For a team lead, this converts a vague "let's do AI" into a portfolio discipline: screen the backlog with the Opportunity Framework, match each surviving idea to the lowest sufficient pattern, and enforce small/real/visible/demo-able scoping. That process reliably produces finishable, high-signal projects — the kind that build capability and belief rather than expensive disappointment.

Forward this to your team as a build rubric: screen with the Opportunity Framework, start at Pattern 1, and scope small/real/visible/demo-able. That's a repeatable recipe for wins.
