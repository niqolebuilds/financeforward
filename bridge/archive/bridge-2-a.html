Bridge 2 — Think like a builder

Subject Why you start with the simplest possible build

Four ways to build, from very simple to more involved — and you almost always start with the simplest one.

When people first hear they can build with AI, they often picture the most complicated version — a sophisticated autonomous system wiring itself into every hospital database (Figure B2.1). Then they feel overwhelmed and decide it's not for them. So let me do the opposite: let me show you that there are four ways to build, they range from very simple to more involved, and you almost always start with the simplest one. Thinking like a builder is mostly about picking the right-sized tool.

Pattern one is a simple AI feature: prompt in, useful output. You give the AI some text and it gives you something helpful back — summarize this denial, draft a reply to this payer, explain this policy in plain language. That's it. Most great hackathon projects are just this, done well. Start here, always. You'd be amazed how much a well-crafted prompt-in, answer-out tool can do for a real workflow.

Pattern two is RAG, which sounds technical but means something simple: grounding the AI's answers in our own documents. Instead of the AI answering from general knowledge, it answers from our actual policies, our payer contracts, our procedures — and it can cite where it got each answer. Imagine asking a question and getting a reply based on our real manual, with the page reference. That's RAG, and it's perfect for the "answer the same policy question for the fifth time" problems.

Pattern three is agents: give a goal, and the AI plans and takes several steps using tools. This is the "work these denials" example we've used all series — goal in, multi-step work out, with you reviewing. Pattern four is MCP, the universal connector — think of it as USB-C for AI, a standard way to let an agent safely reach our systems. You don't need to master three and four to start. You just need to know the ladder exists, so you can climb it only as far as your problem requires.

Here's the scoping trick that makes projects actually finishable (Figure B2.2). Aim for something SMALL, REAL, VISIBLE, and DEMO-ABLE. Small: one workflow, not your whole job. Real: a problem you actually have, not a hypothetical. Visible: something people feel the pain of, so the win lands. Demo-able: something you can show working live in a few minutes. A project that fits all four is a project you can finish in a hackathon and be proud of — and it beats an ambitious idea you never get working.

So thinking like a builder isn't about being technical (Figure B2.3). It's about looking at your annoying task and asking two questions: what's the simplest pattern that could help (usually number one), and how do I scope this small enough to actually demo it? Start simple, stay small, and layer up only if the problem truly needs it. That's the entire craft, and you already have the judgment for it — it's the same judgment you use to right-size everything else in your work.
