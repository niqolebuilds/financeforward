Bridge 3 — You belong here

Subject The quiet voice telling you that you don't belong — and why it's wrong

You do not need to know how to code. The valuable person in the room is the one who understands the problem.

Let me guess what's stopping you. Somewhere in the back of your mind is a quiet voice saying, "A hackathon is for the tech people. I'd be out of my depth. I'd embarrass myself." I want to answer that voice directly and kindly, because it's wrong, and it's the only thing standing between you and something you'd genuinely enjoy. So here's the truth, plainly: you belong at this hackathon, exactly as you are.

Start with the biggest myth. You do not need to know how to code. Modern no-code tools do the heavy lifting — you describe what you want in plain language and they build it. The valuable person in a hackathon isn't the one who can write software; it's the one who deeply understands the problem worth solving. That's you. You know exactly which denial is painful, which report is a slog, which question gets asked over and over. That knowledge is the rare ingredient. The building is the easy part now.

Next myth: that you might do it wrong. There are no wrong answers in a hackathon. The entire point is to learn by doing, not to ship perfection. A prototype that half-works taught you something. An idea that flops taught the room something. Nobody is judging you against a standard of expertise; everyone is a beginner at this, including the people who look confident. It's a workshop for the curious, not an exam for the qualified.

Let me demystify the day itself, so it feels less like a cliff (Figure B3.1). A hackathon has a friendly rhythm: a kickoff where you form teams and share ideas, a working session where you build with support and mentors nearby, a show-and-tell where each team demos, and light judging at the end. That's it. It's more like a lively workshop than a high-pressure competition. There are starter kits, there are people to help, and there's a lot of energy in the room. You will not be left alone to figure it out.

And when it's your turn to show what you made, there's a simple story that always works (Figure B3.2). Show the pain — the annoying task as it is today. Show it working — your tool, live. Show one number — the time it could save, or a rough estimate of impact. Pain, proof, payoff. You don't need a polished pitch; you need to make the room feel the problem and see that your thing helps. Anyone can tell that three-part story, especially about a task they know intimately.

So here's my warm, low-stakes invitation (Figure B3.3). Our Siloam AI hackathon is this December. You don't need to prepare much — just bring the annoying task you've been carrying and an open mind. Jot down one idea this week. Consider signing up. Come be a beginner alongside the rest of us, and find out how much you can build in a day. The room is better with you in it, precisely because you know the problems that matter. You belong there. I hope you'll come.
