Bridge 1 — Your issues are ideas

Subject The task Rahmi dreaded, reframed as an idea

A denial she's redrafted a hundred times turns out to be the best hackathon idea she has — once someone tells her to look at it differently.

Rahmi has a task she genuinely dreads. Every week, she works through a pile of the same low-dollar denials — reading each one, checking it against the payer's rule, drafting the same kind of appeal she's written a hundred times. It's not hard. It's just soul-wearingly repetitive, and it eats a chunk of every week she'd rather spend on the accounts that actually need her brain. For years she thought of it as just part of the job — a headache to endure.

Then someone reframed it for her with one sentence, and it changed everything: "Rahmi, that's not just an annoying task. That's an idea. That's a thing you could build a tool to help with." She'd never thought of her own frustration as raw material for a solution. But that's exactly what it is. The tasks you find most tedious are usually the ones most worth automating — and nobody understands them better than the person who does them every week.

This is the whole mindset shift I want to plant in this bridge: your daily issues are ideas in disguise (Figure B1.1). Every denial you dread, every reconciliation you chase, every payer question you answer for the fifth time — each one is a buildable opportunity wearing the costume of a chore. The move is from problem-spotter to solution-seeker: instead of just enduring the friction, you start asking, "what if something could help with this?"

And here's a simple filter to know when the answer is probably yes. If a task mostly involves reading, writing, or deciding, AI can likely help (Figure B1.2). Reading a claim or a policy? That's reading. Drafting an appeal or a summary? That's writing. Routing, matching, or prioritizing? That's deciding. Rahmi's dreaded denial work is all three at once — which is exactly why it's such a good candidate. Hold your most annoying task up to that filter and see how many of the three it hits.

It helps to have a mental model for what these tools actually do, so they feel less like magic. Think of AI as Understand + Reason + Act. It can understand information (read the denial), reason about it (figure out which appeal argument fits), and act (draft the letter and queue it for you). That's it. Not magic — just those three things, done tirelessly, on the boring parts, with you still making every real decision. Once you see it that way, you start spotting Understand-Reason-Act opportunities all over your week.

Rahmi's dreaded denials are now the idea she's bringing to the hackathon (Figure B1.3). She hasn't built anything yet, and she's not an engineer — but she has something more important than either: she knows the problem cold, and she's stopped seeing it as just a chore. That shift, from enduring to imagining, is the only prerequisite for everything that comes next. You already have the ideas. You've been complaining about them for years.
