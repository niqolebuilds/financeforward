Issue 3 — Anatomy of an Agent: reasoning, memory, tools, autonomy

Subject Open the box: the four parts of an agent, and where you sit

Reasoning, memory, tools, autonomy — what each does, what it's worth, and how the human gate keeps it safe.

Dewi, one of our AP analysts, admitted she'd been avoiding the word "agent" — it sounded like a black box she'd be accountable for without understanding. So we opened it, and found four plain parts. Reasoning: it breaks a goal into steps. Memory: it holds context across the task — this vendor's quirks, last month's exceptions. Tools: it reaches into real systems and acts. Autonomy: it takes the next step on its own, within limits you set. "It's not magic," she said. "It's an organised assistant." Exactly.

Those four parts are why our throughline is literally true: a chatbot answers; an agent acts. A chatbot has reasoning and words and stops there. An agent adds memory, tools, and bounded autonomy — the parts that let it finish work instead of just describing it.

That anatomy is also the business case. Because it has tools and autonomy, one agent can run 3,000+ claim-status checks a day — the equivalent of 5–8 FTEs, about $350K a year (solventum.com; healthcarefinancenews.com) — while memory keeps quality steady across every repetition. A pure chatbot can explain a denial code and save minutes; it can't open the portal, so minutes are its ceiling. The value lives in the acting parts; the reasoning part just aims them.

Now the part that keeps it safe, because acting at that scale demands it. Autonomy is bounded on purpose (Figure 3.2): the agent reads, checks, and drafts at machine speed, then stops at a human-in-the-loop gate. A named person approves before anything is submitted, and every step runs under a scoped "agent identity" — its own credentials, its own log. This isn't ceremony: ~88% of organizations have had an AI-related security incident, yet only ~22% give agents proper identity and access controls (accelirate.com). The agent-identity discipline is the difference between traceable and reckless — and it's squarely in our wheelhouse.

So here's how to put it to work. Take one workflow and name its four parts: the goal (reasoning), what it must remember (memory), which systems it touches (tools), and where you place the gate (autonomy). Deploy the value-creating parts at volume, bound the risky one with the human gate, instrument it with an agent identity, and measure payback where it shows — review time per item, cost per task, throughput. Know the anatomy and you can size the return and govern it in the same breath. The grind goes to the machine; the judgment — what's actually correct — stays with you.

Your next step: Map one of your workflows to the four parts and reply with it. Bring your three sharpest candidates; in Issue 4 we go concrete on agents in the revenue cycle and start turning these maps into a real shortlist.
