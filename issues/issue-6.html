Issue 6 — Agents in treasury, fraud, and compliance

Subject Treasury, fraud, compliance: big upside, one piece of homework

A 2 a.m. fraud catch, the work agents do across three domains, and the 88/22 gap that dictates how we deploy.

Rina, in our treasury and controls function, describes the night an odd payment ran — strange amount, unusual beneficiary, a time no one's at a desk. Old world: it waits in a log for days. Instead, an agent scored it anomalous, held it, and alerted. By morning the flag was waiting with its reasoning attached. Crucially, the agent held and alerted — it did not reverse anything. The decision stayed with Rina.

That small story spans three jobs, and the throughline holds across all of them: a chatbot answers; an agent acts. In treasury: sweep balances, forecast cash, flag idle funds. In fraud: watch transactions, score anomalies, freeze-and-alert. In compliance: screen vendors, check policy, assemble audit evidence (Figure 6.1).

The upside is measurable — faster detection, full coverage instead of sampled, audit packs that assemble themselves — but one number dictates how we deploy: about 88% of organizations have had an AI-related security incident, while only ~22% treat their agents as identity-bearing with proper access controls (accelirate.com; Figure 6.2). In functions that move money and face regulators, that gap is the risk. So the rule here is identity first, autonomy second.

Concretely, every agent in these domains carries scoped, least-privilege credentials, a complete and immutable action log, and no unilateral authority over money movement or regulatory calls. It watches, scores, and assembles; a human approves the release, the block, or the filing. That human-in-the-loop gate is what makes a regulated deployment signable — and Rina's ability to see exactly what the agent looked at, and that it touched nothing it shouldn't, is the point, not a nicety.

Execution sequence we'd recommend: (1) stand up agent identity — scoped credentials plus logging — before anything goes live; (2) start in watch-and-alert mode, flagging but never acting on money movement; (3) measure detection latency, coverage, and false-positive rate against a baseline; (4) widen autonomy only where the log proves reliability, always keeping the human approval gate on consequential actions. Do the homework first and this becomes one of the safest, highest-leverage places to let an agent work.

And the human note to close on: agents don't replace the judgment that makes people good at these jobs — the nose for what smells wrong, the read on an evasive vendor, the call on whether to hold a payment. Those stay yours. What changes is that you no longer have to be awake at 2 a.m. to catch it. The watching scales; the deciding stays human — and fear of the unknown shrinks once you can see exactly what the agent did and where you still hold the gate.

Your next step: Pick one "always-on watcher" job in your area — a payment pattern, a limit, a policy breach — and reply with it. Watch-and-alert is the safest first step, and we'll help you frame the identity-first setup before anything goes live.
