Issue 7 — Human-in-the-loop: accountability without losing control

Subject A dial, a gate, and a badge: how accountability survives automation

The quiet question — "who's responsible when the agent does it?" — answered with autonomy levels, a human gate, and agent identity, plus three steps to govern your first pilot.

Six issues in, we've shown agents doing real finance work. Under every example is one honest question, usually asked quietly: if the agent does it, who's responsible? This issue answers it — with three controls you already understand.

First, a dial, not a switch (Figure 7.1). Autonomy runs from suggest-only, to draft-for-approval, to act-only-after-sign-off, up to bounded full autonomy reserved for narrow, low-risk tasks. Most finance work lives in the middle: the agent prepares, a person approves. You set the level per workflow — you're never handed an uncontrollable black box.

Second, the gate. For anything consequential, a named human approves before execution and owns the outcome. When Ana approves an agent-drafted appeal, Ana is accountable — exactly as if a junior colleague had drafted it. The agent doesn't dilute responsibility; it changes what the responsible person spends time on.

Third, a badge. Every agent should carry its own identity — scoped credentials, not a shared login — so every action is logged and attributable. This is the biggest gap in practice: ~88% of organizations have had an AI-related security incident, but only ~22% treat agents as identity-bearing (Figure 7.2; accelirate.com, 2026). We intend to be in that 22% from day one.

Notice the reframe this gives leadership: the risk isn't "agents are uncontrollable," it's "agents without a level, a gate, and an identity are uncontrollable." Each is a checklist item, not a leap of faith. And here's the deeper truth this series will keep returning to — this is a reinvention of how the work is done, and reinvention asks us to lead through some uncertainty rather than wait for perfect certainty. Fear and excitement will sit side by side; the job is to keep fear from freezing us, and good governance is exactly what makes it safe to move.

Three steps to govern your first pilot:

  1. Set the level. Write down the autonomy level for the task — most start at draft-for-approval.
  2. Name the approver. Put a specific person at the gate for consequential actions, and make the sign-off visible.
  3. Issue the badge. Give the agent its own scoped credentials and confirm its actions are logged before go-live.

A dial you set, a gate you pass, a badge that logs — put them together and "who's responsible?" has a clean answer: the human, with better tools and a cleaner trail than before.

Your next step: Reply with one task and the level you'd set it at — it's the most useful sentence you can send us as we design pilots.
