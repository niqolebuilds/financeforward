Issue 1 — Chatbot vs. Agent: what "agentic" really means

Subject A chatbot answers. An agent acts. Here's why that changes our work.

The one-line difference behind agentic AI — told through a Siloam denial, backed by the numbers, with how we'd actually run it.

Ratna in our Lippo Village billing office typed a familiar question into a chatbot last week: "What's the denial reason on this account?" It answered instantly — reason code, plain English, a policy link. Then she did what she always does: opened the payer portal, checked status, pulled the record, drafted the appeal, resubmitted. The tool answered; Ratna did the work. The next day she gave an agentic tool a goal instead of a question — "work this denied claim toward resubmission" — and it checked the live status, gathered the documentation, drafted the appeal, and handed her the packet to approve. Same starting point, completely different kind of help.

That's the whole series in one line: a chatbot answers; an agent acts. A chatbot is a brilliant reference desk. An agent is a diligent junior colleague who can pick up a task, use your systems, and carry it most of the way — while you set the goal and keep the final say.

The reason this matters to finance leadership isn't philosophical, it's on the balance sheet. Gartner projects about 40% of enterprise apps will embed task-specific agents in 2026, up from under 5% in 2025 (via Deloitte). Because an agent produces outcomes rather than words, that capability shows up in the numbers we actually manage — cycle time, cost per task, throughput — not just in a happier help desk. Structured, auditable, high-volume work is exactly agents' strong suit, which is why revenue-cycle queues like Ratna's are such natural first ground.

Here's how we'd actually run it, so "it acts" never means "it's loose." Three things travel together on every workflow. One, autonomy is a dial set per task (Figure 1.2): suggest, act-with-review, or act-with-oversight — you pick the rung. Two, a human-in-the-loop gate stays in place: the agent prepares and proposes, a named person approves, nothing consequential goes out unreviewed. Three, the agent works under a scoped "agent identity" — its own credentials and a full log — so every action is auditable like any system access. Start one workflow at act-with-review, watch the log for two weeks, then decide whether to widen the dial.

And notice what stays put. Ratna is still the professional of record. Her judgment about which denials are worth fighting, and how to word the hard ones, is precisely what the agent defers to. The grind moved off her plate; the expertise stayed on it. That's the trade at the heart of everything ahead — and it's a good one.

Your next step: Pick one repetitive, rule-bound task you'd hand to a careful junior colleague and reply with it. We'll use real Siloam examples to shape where a first pilot could earn its keep — and in Issue 2, I'll show you why finance is already leading this shift.
