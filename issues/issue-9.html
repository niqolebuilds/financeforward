Issue 9 — From change management to change leadership

Subject Trade certainty for curiosity: leading a change with no fixed destination

Why the old A-to-B playbook fails on AI, the C4 reinvention that replaces it, and three moves for leaders — grounded in the honest sentence that makes it work.

Every finance leader has run a change project: new ERP, new close calendar, new policy. The playbook is familiar — define destination B, build the plan, manage people from A to B. It works because B is known and fits on a slide.

Agentic AI breaks that playbook (Figure 9.1). This isn't A to a known B. The tools change every few weeks, the best ways to work with them are still being discovered, and the end-state genuinely can't be drawn in advance. McKinsey calls it a "C4 reinvention" — a move from A into an unknown — and you can't manage your way to a destination you can't yet see. You have to lead toward it.

The data says this is the real work, not a philosophical aside: leaders rank change management and siloed ways of working as bigger barriers to scaling AI than technology infrastructure (McKinsey, Aug 2026). If tools were the constraint, more tool spend would fix it. It isn't, and it doesn't. How the change is led is the constraint.

Leading looks different when you can't point at B (Figure 9.2). Certainty becomes honesty: "I don't know exactly where we're going, but I'm learning alongside you" — a sentence that sounds like weakness in the old model and is the most credible thing a leader can say in this one. Mistakes become curriculum; the fumble you had with an agent last week is what the team needs to hear. Compliance ("because I said so") becomes capability ("let's get good at this together"). And a fixed rollout becomes experiments, where each try produces evidence for the next — which also happens to de-risk better than a rigid plan the next release would invalidate.

This connects straight to last issue's fears: "stepping into the unknown" isn't a problem to eliminate, it's the honest condition of a reinvention, and naming it is how leaders keep fear from freezing the team. Fear and excitement coexist; leadership's job is momentum, not the elimination of doubt.

Three moves for anyone who leads people:

  1. Say the sentence. Tell your team you're learning this alongside them. Curiosity, not certainty, is the credibility move now.
  2. Turn a mistake into a lesson. Share one thing that didn't work and what it taught you; make fumbling safe.
  3. Run one experiment. Pick a small pilot, frame it as a test that produces evidence, and let the result shape the next step.

The relief in all this: you don't have to have the answers, because nobody does yet. Your job is to keep the team learning, safe, and moving while the picture sharpens.

Your next step: Reply and tell us how the sentence lands when you try it — or, if you don't lead a team yet, what you'd want to hear your manager say.
