Issue 13 — Enforce: redesign the operating system, measure better not busier

Subject Enforce: measure better, not busier

Why the last stage of change is redesigning what we measure and reward — and the simple test that keeps the machine from quietly winning.

I once watched a team do everything right — they learned the new agent-assisted way, believed in it, got genuinely better for a few months. Then the old ways crept back. Not from any decision, but because everything around them still rewarded the old behavior: the metrics counted old outputs, the praise went to whoever stayed latest, promotion still favored doing it all by hand. The system quietly pulled them back. That's the problem the last stage of the playbook solves.

It's called Enforce, which is a bad name, because it isn't about forcing anyone. It's about redesigning the system so the new way is the natural way. If you change how people work but not what you measure, reward, and promote, the system wins and the change loses. So Enforce means updating the whole operating model: metrics, incentives, decision rights, promotion criteria, and the small weekly rituals that shape how a team actually behaves.

Measure better, not busier. The bravest and most concrete piece is measurement. For decades we measured busyness — hours, volume, tickets closed. When an agent can process near-infinite volume, those metrics turn harmful, because they reward the machine and frame the human as its losing competitor. We have to measure better, not busier: cycle time, quality and error rates, value per workflow, cost per task, and human-review burden — not logins, licenses, or prompt counts. Here's a simple test: if a tireless machine could max out a metric by doing low-value work, it's the wrong metric. The right ones require human judgment and value, which a machine alone can't fake.

The metric is a message. Keep measuring volume and you tell people the machine is their competitor. Measure quality, judgment, and value and you tell them the machine is their tool and their thinking is the point. Decision rights and promotion carry the same message even louder — if authority and advancement still flow to those who do everything by hand, no dashboard will hold the new behavior. Reward the skills the new model needs (judgment, process redesign, agent oversight) and you align the organization's strongest incentives with the change.

Rituals do quiet, heavy lifting too. The weekly meeting that reviewed volume can review what we learned and improved. The recognition that celebrated the latest-night hero can celebrate whoever redesigned a process so nobody has to stay late. Repeated small signals are how a new way stops being an initiative and becomes "how we do things here." None of this costs much money; it costs will, because it changes what gets rewarded — including for the leaders who succeeded under the old rules.

This is the hardest stage, and the one that decides whether everything before it holds. If we've asked people to learn, believe, and grow, we owe them a system that rewards it — otherwise we're asking them to swim against a current we control. Get this right and the change becomes permanent and self-sustaining. Next, before we imagine the destination, we turn to the people who carry the heaviest load in getting there: our middle managers.
