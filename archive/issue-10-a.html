Issue 10 — The 1:3:5 rule: the technology is the cheap part

Subject The tool was the easy part

Why the agent license is the smallest line on the invoice — and where the real work hides.

A colleague came back from a conference thrilled: "The agent platform is cheaper than I thought — we could just buy it." I get the excitement. But it reminded me of buying a treadmill. The machine is the easy purchase. The results come from everything that's hard to buy: the habit, the routine, the change in how you actually live.

McKinsey studied where the money and effort really go in AI transformations and found a clean pattern — call it the 1:3:5 rule (Figure 10.1). For every $1 you spend on the technology itself, expect about $3 on redesigning the process around it, and about $5 on building capability and driving adoption. The tool is one-ninth of the story. The other eight-ninths is people and process.

Think back to Ana's self-triaging worklist. Buying the agent was the $1. The $3 was rethinking the whole denial workflow so a triaged list actually flows to the right person with the right context. The $5 was helping Ana and her teammates trust it, learn it, and reshape their day around it — plus the coaching, the office hours, the patient answering of "but what about…" The value never lived in the license. It lived in the eight dollars around it.

Here's why this matters even if you never sign a purchase order: it explains why adoption can't be rushed or ordered. If capability and behavior change are literally five times the cost of the tool, then the team's learning isn't overhead getting in the way of the rollout — it is the rollout. The human-in-the-loop review, the training, the honest conversations about fear from Issue 8 — that's not friction. That's the main event.

The trap most organizations fall into is inverting the ratio (Figure 10.2) — pouring money into tools and treating adoption as a memo. That's how you end up with expensive software nobody uses. We'd rather spend where the value actually is.

So if the pace of this series has felt deliberate — explaining, proving, naming fears before rushing to deploy — now you know why. We're spending our "eight dollars" on purpose.

Your next step: Think of one place a tool got bought but never really adopted. Reply with what you think was missing — process, capability, or trust. Your hunch will help us spend the eight dollars well.
