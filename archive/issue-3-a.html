Issue 3 — Anatomy of an Agent: reasoning, memory, tools, autonomy

Subject What's actually under the hood

Once Dewi saw the four parts of an agent, it stopped feeling like magic and started feeling like a colleague.

Dewi, one of our AP analysts, told me she'd avoided the word "agent" because it sounded like a black box she'd be blamed for. So we opened the box together, and it turned out to have just four parts. By the end she said the thing I hoped she would: "Oh — it's not magic. It's a very organised assistant."

Part one is reasoning: the agent breaks a goal into steps. Give it "reconcile this vendor statement" and it plans — pull our ledger, line up the statement, match invoices, flag what doesn't tie. Part two is memory: it holds context, so it remembers this vendor's quirks, the last exceptions you resolved, the format their statements come in. Part three is tools: it can actually reach into systems — the ledger, the payer portal, email — and do things there, not just talk about them. Part four is autonomy: it can take the next step on its own, within limits you set.

Put those together and our line finally makes mechanical sense: a chatbot answers because it only has the reasoning-and-words part; an agent acts because it also has memory, tools, and bounded autonomy. That's the difference between a colleague who can tell you what to do and one who can help do it.

Here's the part that settled Dewi's worry. Autonomy is bounded on purpose, and there's a gate where it matters (Figure 3.2). In a claim workflow the agent can read, check status, and draft the appeal — thousands a day if needed — but it stops at a human gate before anything is submitted. A named person approves. And it all happens under an "agent identity": the agent has its own scoped credentials and every action is logged, so if anyone ever asks "who did this and why," there's a clean answer. That's not a black box. That's more traceable than a busy afternoon of manual clicks.

Once you can name the parts, the fear of the unknown shrinks to something manageable. Reasoning plans it, memory remembers it, tools do it, autonomy carries it — and you hold the gate. Dewi's takeaway was exactly right: not magic, just an organised assistant that happens to be tireless. And the judgment about what's actually correct? That stayed hers.

Your next step: Pick one task and name its four parts: what's the goal (reasoning), what would the agent need to remember (memory), which systems would it touch (tools), and where would you put the human gate (autonomy)? Reply with one — I'll feature a few reader-mapped workflows next time.
