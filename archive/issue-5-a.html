Issue 5 — Agents in FP&A and the month-end close

Subject The close that stopped eating the weekend

Adi's team used to lose the first week of every month to the close. This month they got it back.

Adi leads FP&A at one of our entities, and for as long as anyone remembers, the first week of the month belonged to the close. Pull the data from three systems. Reconcile. Chase the entries that don't tie. Book the adjustments. Only then — usually around day eight, often over a weekend — could the team actually look at the numbers and say something useful about them. The analysis, the part everyone trained for, always got the least time and the most tired brains.

This month, an agent took the repetitive middle (Figure 5.1). It pulled the data from each system on schedule, ran the first-pass reconciliations, matched what matched, and drafted the routine journal entries. What didn't tie, it flagged. When Adi's team arrived, they weren't starting from raw extracts — they were starting from a drafted close with a clean list of exceptions. Day eight became roughly day four.

Notice the shape of it: a chatbot answers; an agent acts. A chatbot could explain a variance if you asked. The agent assembled the reconciliation, drafted the entries, and handed the team a close that was most of the way done — then waited for review.

And review is exactly where the humans stayed. Every drafted journal entry sat behind a human gate; nothing posted until an analyst approved it, and the sign-off stayed with the people accountable for the numbers. The agent's work ran under its own scoped identity with a full log, which — for anyone who's ever rebuilt an audit trail by hand — is its own small miracle. The agent did the assembling; the team did the judging and the signing.

The best part wasn't the four days saved. It was what those days became (Figure 5.2). Instead of spending the month-end buried in prep, Adi's analysts spent it on the story behind the numbers — why the margin moved, what the forecast should say, which department needed a conversation. One analyst said the close finally felt like analysis instead of assembly. That's the shift: the same close, but the human hours pointed at judgment instead of janitorial work.

A calmer close isn't a luxury. A team that isn't exhausted by day eight catches more, explains better, and forecasts with a clearer head. The grind moved; the thinking stayed — and got more room to breathe.

Your next step: Which step of your close eats the most hours for the least judgment — the data pull, the reconciliations, the routine entries? Reply with the one you'd most like to hand off; it's the natural place to start.
