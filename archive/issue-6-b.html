Issue 6 — Agents in treasury, fraud, and compliance

Subject 88% had an AI security incident. Only 22% control for it. Mind the gap.

A held payment at 2 a.m. shows the upside; one statistic shows the homework that makes it safe.

A treasury analyst got an overnight fraud flag with the reasoning already attached — the upside is obvious; the number that should shape how we deploy is less comforting.

Here's the stat every leader scaling agents in treasury, fraud, or compliance needs on the wall: about 88% of organizations have already experienced an AI-related security incident, yet only ~22% treat their agents as identity-bearing with proper access controls (accelirate.com; Figure 6.2). The capability is spreading faster than the controls around it — and in functions that move money and answer to regulators, that gap is the whole risk.

The upside is real and worth naming first. Across three domains (Figure 6.1), agents do genuine work: in treasury, continuous balance sweeps, cash forecasting, and idle-fund flags; in fraud, always-on transaction monitoring, anomaly scoring, and freeze-and-alert; in compliance, vendor screening, policy checks, and audit-evidence assembly. Because a chatbot answers while an agent acts, these show up as faster detection, fewer missed anomalies, and audit packs that assemble themselves — measurable in detection latency and analyst hours, not survey scores.

But the 88/22 gap dictates the deployment order: identity first, autonomy second. Every agent should carry its own scoped credentials (least-privilege access), a complete and immutable action log, and — critically in these domains — no unilateral authority over money movement or regulatory decisions. The agent watches, scores, and assembles; a human approves the release, the block, or the filing. That human-in-the-loop gate isn't friction; in a regulated function it's the control that makes the whole thing deployable.

For the analytically minded, the ROI has two sides that must be weighed together. Upside: reduced detection latency, broader coverage (an agent watches 100% of transactions, not a sample), and lower manual audit-prep cost. Downside if you skip the identity work: you join the 88% — and in treasury or compliance, a single incident can dwarf the efficiency gains. The expected-value case is strongly positive only when the identity controls are in place before you widen autonomy.

Recommended sequence: (1) stand up agent identity — scoped credentials plus logging — before any live deployment; (2) start in "watch and alert" mode, where the agent flags but never acts on money movement; (3) measure detection latency, coverage, and false-positive rate against baseline; (4) widen autonomy only where the log proves reliability, always keeping the human approval gate on consequential actions. Do the homework first, and this is one of the safest, highest-leverage places agents earn their keep. Forward this to whoever owns treasury or compliance risk.
