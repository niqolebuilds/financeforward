Issue 9 — From change management to change leadership

Subject From having the answers to leading the learning

Agentic AI is a reinvention, not a migration — and reinventions need leaders who learn out loud alongside their teams.

Most of us learned to lead change by having the answers: here's the new process, here's the training, here's the path from A to B. It worked because B was known. What makes this moment different is that we can't fully see B. Agentic AI is a reinvention of how work gets done, not a migration to a known destination — and reinventions require change leadership, not just change management.

The distinction is real. Change management assumes a known destination, a plan, and a push for compliance. Change leadership assumes an unknown destination, learning in the open, and building capability as you go. You cannot "roll out" a place you can't yet see; you can only lead people toward it by learning together. Apply rollout mechanics to a reinvention and you get the exact failure the research names — flawless plans that still stall because people don't trust, learn, or adopt.

The most honest sentence a leader can say. Here is the sentence I'd put at the center of this whole issue: "I don't know exactly where we're going, but I'm learning alongside you." Every instinct we were trained on says project certainty. But false certainty about an unknown destination is theater, and people can tell. Admitting you're learning doesn't diminish you — it makes it safe for everyone else to learn, which is the only way a team moves into unfamiliar terrain.

Day to day, leading this way means treating experiments as evidence and mistakes as curriculum. When someone tries an agent on a task and it half-works, that's not a failure to bury — it's data to share. The leader's job shifts from having answers to designing good experiments, removing obstacles, and making sure one person's lesson becomes everyone's. It's cheaper (small bets), faster (parallel learning), and more durable (capability compounds) than a monolithic rollout — and it's the configuration the evidence ties to actually realizing returns.

The data even rewards the posture: employees at high-performing organizations are about 3x more likely to say their leaders own the AI agenda — where ownership means visibly leading the learning, not having the answers. That behavior costs dollars nothing and ego something, and it's what separates organizations that capture AI value from those that merely buy it.

So this issue is an invitation, especially to my fellow managers: trade the pressure to have answers for the courage to learn out loud. We don't need the whole road. We need to model curiosity, share what we learn, keep each other safe, and take the next honest step together. That isn't a lesser leadership. In a reinvention, it's the only kind that works — and it's the bridge into the back half of this series, where we get practical about how to lead the change well.
