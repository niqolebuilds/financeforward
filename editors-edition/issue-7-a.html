Issue 7 — Human-in-the-loop: accountability without losing control

Subject The quiet question in the room

When an agent drafts the work, who's on the hook if it's wrong? Here's the design that answers it — and why the fear goes quiet once you see it.

When our team first saw an agent draft a batch of appeals, the room got a little quiet, and then someone asked the question everyone was thinking: "If the machine does the work, who's on the hook when it's wrong?" It's the right question, and I was glad it came out loud rather than staying a worry in people's chests. Because the answer is not a shrug or a technicality. It's the entire point of how we deploy these things.

The answer is: you are, and that's by design. An agent proposes; a human reviews; a human approves; only then does the action happen. The agent never takes the last step alone. If it drafts an appeal, you read it before it's sent. If it prepares an entry, you approve it before it posts. Accountability doesn't move to the machine, because the machine never gets the final say.

People sometimes hear "human-in-the-loop" and picture it as a brake — something that slows a fast tool down. I'd ask you to see it the other way. It's the thing that lets us use a fast tool at all. Without a review step, an agent's speed would be terrifying in a hospital; with one, that speed becomes safe, because every consequential action still crosses a desk where a person can catch what the machine missed. The loop isn't distrust of the technology. It's the design that earns our trust.

There's a second safeguard worth knowing, because it protects you as much as the patient. The agent is treated as its own identity in our systems — a name, tightly limited access, and a log of everything it does. So if a question ever arises about what happened on an account, we can show exactly what the agent saw and did, right next to what the human decided. That trail doesn't just catch errors; it protects the good-faith professional who was doing everything right.

Here's what I've watched happen once people understand this: the fear goes quiet. Not because we told them not to worry, but because they can see the guardrail with their own eyes and put their hands on it. Control didn't leave the room; it got a tireless assistant. The research backs the feeling, too — teams that trust how a tool is governed are markedly less anxious about it, and that trust starts with being able to see the safeguard clearly.

So if the quiet question is sitting in your chest right now — who's responsible? — let me answer it plainly and warmly: you still are, and the whole system is built so that you can be. The agent carries the work. You keep the judgment, the accountability, and the last click. That has not changed, and we are not going to change it.
