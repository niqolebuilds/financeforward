Issue 11 — Awareness & Belief: how change actually starts

Subject Awareness isn't belief — and belief is the real work

You can announce a new tool with a memo. You cannot make people believe in it that way — belief only travels person to person.

Here's something I've learned the slow way: you can create awareness with a memo, but you cannot create belief with one. Awareness is "I know we're doing this AI thing." Belief is "I actually think this could help me, and it's safe to try." Those are different states, and the gap between them is where most change quietly dies. A team can be fully aware of a new tool and completely unmoved by it. Getting from aware to believing is the real work of these early stages.

And belief doesn't travel top-down; it travels sideways. People don't come to believe because leadership announced something. They come to believe when they watch a peer they respect try the tool, survive, and quietly get better at their job. That's why champions matter so much more than mandates. One trusted colleague saying "I tried this and it actually saved me an hour" moves more belief than ten leadership emails.

But — and this is the part leaders sometimes skip — belief only spreads if leaders go first. Not by decree, by example. If I'm asking the team to try something new and unfamiliar, and I haven't touched it myself, the ask rings hollow. When people see their leader fumbling through the same tool, learning out loud, being a visible beginner, it does two things: it makes the tool feel safe, and it makes being a learner feel respectable. Leaders adopt first not to look expert, but to make it okay to not be.

So the sequence that actually works is human, not mechanical. Leaders try it in the open and share what they learn. A few early champions — often not the most senior people, just the curious and trusted ones — pick it up and tell their honest stories. Their peers watch, feel it's safe, and step in. Belief spreads like warmth through a room, person to person, until one day it's just how the team works. You can't shortcut that with urgency; you can only tend it.

I want to name the emotional truth underneath this, because it's tender. Trying something new in front of colleagues is exposing. People worry about looking slow, or foolish, or replaceable. The whole point of leaders-first and champions-next is to make that exposure safe — to prove, by example, that being a beginner here is normal and okay. When the research says low-trust teams are far more anxious, this is the antidote: not more information, but more visible, safe, human examples of trying and learning.

So if you're a manager reading this, the assignment is gentle but real: be the first to try, be honest about the fumbling, and then find and celebrate your champions. And if you're not a manager, you might be a champion without the title — the person whose honest "I tried this" makes it safe for everyone around you. Belief is built by people like that, one true story at a time.
