Issue 10 — The 1:3:5 rule: technology is the cheap part

Subject 1:3:5 — the one ratio worth carrying into your next planning meeting

Technology is the cheap part. The $3 of process redesign and the $5 of capability are where transformations are actually won.

Two teams buy the same agent for the same task. Team One spends almost everything on the technology and waits for savings. Team Two spends a little on the tool and a lot on redesigning the workflow, training people, and coaching them through the change. A year later Team One has expensive software nobody uses; Team Two has a transformed process. Same tool, opposite outcomes — and one ratio explains why.

It's the 1:3:5 rule: for every $1 on the AI technology, expect roughly $3 on process redesign and $5 on capability-building and adoption. The technology is the cheap part — and most organizations invert the ratio, over-spending on tools and under-spending on people. That inversion is the most common and most expensive mistake in the field.

Why this is good news for a careful team. An agent's value is realized only when the workflow is redesigned to use it and the people around it can run and trust it. Spend heavily on the tool and lightly on those two, and you've bought capability you can't convert — not because the agent is weak, but because nothing around it changed. The technology sets the ceiling; process and capability decide how much of that ceiling you reach. So success doesn't go to whoever spends most on software. It goes to whoever does the human work best — a game a disciplined, thoughtful team can win regardless of budget. The 1:3:5 rule hands the advantage to the careful, not the rich.

This reframes the ROI math and the whole series. The real investment for every $1 of technology is about $9 all-in, and the $8 of process and people is where returns are won or lost. So when I ask for patience to redesign a workflow or time for people to learn, I'm not slowing the project — I'm spending the 89% of the budget that actually determines whether it works. The coaching and change-leadership talk isn't soft accompaniment to the real work; in this frame, it is the real work.

For the metrics-minded, watch the spend mix as a leading indicator. If a program is spending 70% on technology, expect underperformance no matter how good the tools are; a program shaped closer to 1:3:5 is structurally set up to convert. And the corollary is bracing: buying better technology is rarely the fix for a struggling AI program. The fix is almost always redesigning the process and investing in people — the parts that don't demo well but do decide the outcome.

So here's the one number I'd want every person in our directorate to carry into their next planning conversation: 1:3:5. When a proposal is 90% software and 10% everything else, you now know the ratio is upside down and how to right it — fund the people and the process like they're the point, because they are.
