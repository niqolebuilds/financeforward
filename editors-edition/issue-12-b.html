Issue 12 — Commit & Develop: choice, coaching, and the skill partnership

Subject The skill partnership: 57% automatable, 70% enduring

The reskilling math resolves most of the anxiety — and it tells you exactly where capability-building dollars should go.

Behind the reskilling debate is a number pair that resolves most of the anxiety. MGI finds that up to 57% of work hours may be automatable, yet more than 70% of the human skills organizations actually seek are the enduring kind — judgment, communication, problem-solving, relationship-building. Automatable is not the same as automated, and the skills that remain in demand are overwhelmingly human. That gap is the entire investment thesis for capability.

This defines a skill partnership rather than a substitution. If a large fraction of hours can shift to agents while the sought-after skills stay human, the optimal configuration is people doing high-skill work with agents carrying the routine load. The ROI of capability-building is what closes the distance between those two facts — it moves people from the automatable hours into the enduring skills, which is exactly where organizational value concentrates.

On how to build capability, the evidence favors embedded coaching over episodic training and a leveled progression over one-off certification. Think of capability in levels — from Level 1 (aware) to Level 5 (able to set standards and enforce) — with coaching woven into real work moving people up the ladder. Workshops transfer information; embedded coaching transfers capability, because skill forms through repeated, supported practice on live tasks, not through a slide deck.

Commitment has an ROI dimension too, and it's counterintuitive: choice increases it. People commit to changes they help shape and resist changes imposed on them, so widening the zone of choice — real agency over how the change lands in someone's role — raises adoption at essentially no cost. Mandates feel efficient but produce compliance, which decays into quiet non-use; genuine commitment, earned through choice, produces durable adoption. The cheaper-looking path is the more expensive one over any real horizon.

There's a cost the cheerful version omits: role-redesign grief. When part of a valued role moves to an agent, people experience real loss, and unacknowledged loss slows adoption measurably. Budgeting time and space to name it isn't sentimentality — it's throughput. Teams allowed to process the change move through it faster than teams rushed past it, which shows up directly in how quickly capability and adoption ramp.

For measurement, track capability progression (movement up the levels), not training completion; and track commitment (chosen, not compliant use), not attendance. These predict the outcomes that matter. A workforce climbing the capability ladder with genuine commitment is the asset that converts every prior dollar of technology and process spend into results — the payoff end of the 1:3:5 chain.

A last measurement caution that ties this issue to the whole series: beware celebrating the wrong milestones. Training sessions delivered, certifications issued, and licenses assigned all feel like progress, and all can be completely disconnected from value. The metrics that actually predict return are capability progression on real tasks and genuine, chosen use — and both are slower and harder to count than attendance. Leaders under pressure to show momentum drift toward the easy numbers, which is exactly how a program looks busy while going nowhere. Hold the line on measuring capability and commitment, even though they're the harder things to put on a slide. They're the only leading indicators that connect to the cycle-time, quality, and cost outcomes the whole 1:3:5 investment is chasing.

Forward this to whoever owns L&D: fund embedded coaching and leveled capability, not one-off training — that's what converts the rest of the AI budget.
