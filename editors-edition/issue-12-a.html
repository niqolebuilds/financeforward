Issue 12 — Commit & Develop: choice, coaching, and the skill partnership

Subject The choice nobody can make for you

Commitment happens from the inside. What leaders can do is widen the zone of choice, name the grief, and coach people up.

There's a moment in every real change where a person stops going along with it and starts owning it. That moment is a choice, and it can't be skipped or ordered. You can inform someone, you can support them, but you can't commit for them — commitment is something a person does from the inside. The best leaders I've watched understand this, so instead of pushing harder, they widen the zone of choice: they give people real agency in how the change shows up in their own work.

I also want to say something honest that gets left out of cheerful change memos: when a role gets redesigned, there's often grief. If part of your job — a part you were good at, maybe proud of — moves to an agent, it's normal to feel a real sense of loss, even if the new version of the role is better. Pretending that loss isn't there doesn't help anyone. Naming it does. People move through change faster when they're allowed to acknowledge what they're leaving behind, not rushed past it.

Once someone has chosen in, development is what carries them forward — and the kind that works is embedded, not episodic. A one-time training session teaches a tool; coaching that lives inside the actual work teaches a capability. The difference is enormous. People don't build real skill in a workshop; they build it by trying something on a live task, getting a nudge from someone a little further along, adjusting, and trying again. Development that's woven into the work, with a coach nearby, is how capability actually grows.

Here's the reassurance I most want to land, because it speaks straight to the fear of losing your edge. Even as agents take over more tasks, the distinctly human skills — judgment, relationships, knowing what matters — are the ones organizations want more of, not less. The research is striking: a large share of work hours may be automatable, yet the great majority of skills employers actually seek are the enduring, human kind. Your career doesn't shrink as agents grow; it moves up into the work only you can do.

That's why I've started thinking of this as a partnership, not a replacement. You and an agent aren't competing for the same job; you're splitting one. The agent takes the tireless, repetitive half. You keep — and grow into — the judgment half. Development, in this frame, isn't about learning to survive automation. It's about learning to lead a very capable, very narrow assistant, which is a genuinely new and valuable skill in its own right.

So the human arc of this stage is choice, then honesty about loss, then coached growth into a bigger version of your role. None of it is automatic and none of it is fast, and that's okay. We're not processing people through a change; we're helping colleagues we respect grow into work that's more human, not less. That takes patience and care, and it's exactly the kind of thing worth being patient and careful about.
