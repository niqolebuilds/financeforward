Issue 10 — The 1:3:5 rule: technology is the cheap part

Subject Two teams, one agent, opposite outcomes

Why the software is the cheap ninth of the story — and the other eight dollars are what actually decide whether it works.

Let me tell you about two teams — composites, but true to life — that bought the same agent for the same task. Team One spent almost everything on the technology: licenses, setup, a slick demo. They flipped it on and waited for the savings. Team Two spent a little on the technology and a lot on everything around it — redesigning the workflow, training people, coaching them through the change. A year later, Team One had an expensive tool nobody quite used, and Team Two had a genuinely transformed process. Same software. Opposite outcomes.

The pattern behind that difference has a name and a ratio: the 1:3:5 rule. For every $1 you spend on the AI technology itself, expect to spend about $3 on redesigning the process around it and $5 on building people's capability and adoption. The technology is the cheap part. The expensive, decisive part is the human and process work that turns a tool into a result.

This lands hard because it's the opposite of how we instinctively budget. When we buy technology, we think the technology is the thing we're buying. But an agent dropped onto an unchanged process, handed to untrained people, doesn't produce much — not because the agent is weak, but because the workflow around it never changed to let it help. The tool was never the bottleneck. The redesign and the learning were.

I find this genuinely reassuring, and here's why. If success came from buying the best technology, it would go to whoever spent the most. But if success comes from process redesign and capability-building, it goes to whoever does the human work best — and that's a game a disciplined, thoughtful team can win regardless of budget. The 1:3:5 rule says the advantage belongs to the careful, not the rich. That sounds like us.

It also reframes what all the coaching and change-leadership talk in this series is for. It's not soft accompaniment to the "real" technical work. It is the real work — the $5 and the $3 of every $8. When I ask for patience while we redesign a workflow, or time for people to learn, I'm not slowing the project down. I'm spending the part of the budget that actually determines whether it works.

If you're picturing our own directorate, the $3 and the $5 aren't mysterious — they're things we already know how to do. The $3 of process redesign is mapping a workflow, deciding where the agent's step fits, and rebuilding the review checkpoint around it — ordinary process work we do whenever a control changes. The $5 of capability is the coaching, the practice on live tasks, the time for people to get comfortable and confident. Neither requires a data scientist. Both require exactly the patience and rigor finance teams are built for. So when the ratio says the human work is 89% of the job, it's not asking us to become something we're not — it's asking us to do more of what we're already good at, and less shopping for software.

So if you take one number from this whole series into your next planning conversation, make it this one: 1:3:5. When someone proposes an AI project and the plan is 90% software and 10% everything else, you now know the ratio is upside down — and you know how to fix it. Fund the people and the process like they're the point, because they are.
