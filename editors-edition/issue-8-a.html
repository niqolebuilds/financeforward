Issue 8 — The four fears agentic AI stirs, named honestly

Subject The four fears nobody says out loud

Talking about feelings in a finance newsletter, on purpose — because naming a fear is the first step to designing around it.

I want to do something a little unusual for a finance newsletter: talk about feelings. Not because I've gone soft, but because I've noticed that the biggest obstacle to using these tools well isn't technical — it's four unspoken fears, and unspoken is exactly what makes them powerful. So let's name them, plainly and without judgment. I think you'll recognize at least one as your own, and I'll tell you mine.

The first is the fear of being "found out." If an agent can do a chunk of my job, was my expertise ever real? It's a quietly painful thought. The honest reframe: the agent can do the mechanical part of your work precisely because it's mechanical. The part that's actually you — knowing which denial is worth fighting, reading a situation, exercising judgment — is the part it can't do. You weren't faking; you were doing two jobs, and it's taking the one you never should have had to.

The second is accountability without control. I'm responsible for the outcome, but a machine is taking steps — do I still steer? We spent all of last issue on this one, and the answer holds: the human-in-the-loop means you always steer. The agent proposes; you dispose. You are never accountable for an action you didn't approve.

The third is stepping into the unknown. This isn't moving from A to a clear B; it's moving from A to somewhere we can't fully picture yet. That's genuinely uncomfortable, and I won't pretend otherwise. What helps me is remembering that we don't have to see the whole path — we just have to take the next honest step, learn from it, and take another. Nobody has the full map. We're drawing it together.

The fourth is losing your professional edge. Will the skill I spent years building still matter? Here the evidence is actually encouraging: even as more tasks become automatable, the distinctly human skills — judgment, relationships, knowing what matters — are the ones organizations want more of, not less. Your edge doesn't disappear; it moves up the value chain, into the work only a person can do.

Here's why I'm naming these instead of cheerleading past them. A fear you can't say freezes you; a fear you can name, you can design around. Every safeguard in this series — the review step, the identity controls, the start-small approach — exists because someone took one of these fears seriously. Excitement and fear can live in the same chest; that's normal and healthy. The only failure mode is letting the fear go unspoken until it quietly decides for you.
