Issue 10 — The 1:3:5 rule: technology is the cheap part

Subject The 1:3:5 ratio, and the budget mistake behind every disappointing pilot

Most AI programs don't fail on technology. They fail on a budget that spent on the wrong third of the problem.

Behind every disappointing AI pilot is usually a budget that spent on the wrong third of the problem. The ratio that explains it: for every $1 of technology, high-return programs spend about $3 on process redesign and $5 on capability-building and adoption — and most organizations invert this, over-spending on technology and under-spending on people. The inversion is the single most common, most expensive mistake in the field.

The logic is straightforward once stated. An agent's value is realized only when the surrounding workflow is redesigned to use it and the people around it are skilled enough to run and trust it. Spend heavily on the tool and lightly on those two things, and you've bought capability you can't convert. The technology sets the ceiling on possible value; the process and capability spend determines how much of that ceiling you actually reach.

This directly reframes ROI math. The denominator isn't just the license cost — it's the full 1:3:5, roughly $9 of total investment for every $1 of technology. Teams that budget only the $1 are shocked when returns disappoint, because they never funded the $8 that produces the return. Conversely, teams that budget the full stack see the promised gains — and the incremental $8 is cheap relative to the value a working deployment unlocks.

It also tells you where the marginal dollar goes. Once you have adequate technology, the next dollar should almost never buy more technology; it should buy process redesign or capability. This is why the "soft" investments in this series — communication, coaching, role redesign, the change-leadership work — are not soft at all. In the 1:3:5 frame they're 89% of the budget, and empirically they're where the returns are won or lost.

For the metrics-minded, this suggests tracking a spend-mix ratio alongside outcome metrics. If your AI program's actual spending is 70% technology, that's a leading indicator of underperformance regardless of how good the tools are. A program spending in something closer to the 1:3:5 shape is structurally set up to convert. The mix is a diagnostic you can read before the outcomes even arrive.

The uncomfortable but useful implication: buying better technology is rarely the fix for a struggling AI program. The fix is usually redesigning the process and investing in people — the parts that don't demo well but do determine results. For a leader allocating scarce capital, 1:3:5 is less a spending rule than a warning label about where value actually comes from.

A final analytical point that reframes the whole series for a skeptic: this is why the "soft" issues aren't a detour. If capability and adoption are $5 of every $9, then the change-leadership content — the fears, the trust, the coaching, the belief mechanics — is a direct discussion of where 56% of the budget goes and whether it returns. A leader who dismisses that as fluff is, in budget terms, waving away the majority of the investment. The 1:3:5 ratio quietly converts every people-and-process conversation into a financial one. That's the version of this argument that tends to move a CFO: the human work isn't adjacent to the ROI, it is the larger part of the cost base that determines it.

Forward this to whoever signs the AI budget: if the plan is mostly technology, it's structurally set up to underdeliver — rebalance toward the $3 and the $5.
