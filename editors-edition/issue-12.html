Issue 12 — Commit & Develop: choice, coaching, and the skill partnership

Subject Choice, coaching, and the partnership with your agent

The human arc of this stage: choose in, grieve honestly, then grow — coached, on real work — into a bigger version of your role.

Every real change has a moment where a person stops going along with it and starts owning it. That moment is a choice, and it can't be ordered or skipped — commitment is something people do from the inside. The best leaders don't push harder; they widen the zone of choice, giving people real agency over how the change shows up in their own work. Counterintuitively, that agency raises commitment at almost no cost, because people commit to what they help shape and resist what's imposed.

An honest thing left out of cheerful change memos: redesigned roles often bring grief. If part of your job — a part you were good at, maybe proud of — moves to an agent, feeling a real sense of loss is normal, even when the new role is better. Naming that loss helps; pretending it away doesn't. People move through change faster when they're allowed to acknowledge what they're leaving behind rather than being rushed past it.

Development that actually builds capability. Once someone chooses in, development carries them forward — and the kind that works is embedded, not episodic. A one-time session teaches a tool; coaching that lives inside the real work teaches a capability. Picture capability in levels, from Level 1 (aware) to Level 5 (able to set the standard), with coaching moving people up the ladder. Skill forms through supported practice on live tasks — try, get a nudge from someone further along, adjust, try again — not through a slide deck. Everyone starts at Level 1, and that's fine.

Here's the reassurance that speaks straight to the fear of losing your edge. MGI finds up to 57% of work hours may be automatable, yet more than 70% of the skills organizations actually seek are the enduring, human kind — judgment, relationships, knowing what matters. Automatable is not automated, and the skills that stay in demand are overwhelmingly human. Your career doesn't shrink as agents grow; it moves up into the work only you can do. That's why I think of this as a partnership, not a replacement: you and the agent split one job — it takes the tireless repetitive half, you keep and grow the judgment half.

Development in this frame isn't about surviving automation; it's about learning to lead a very capable, very narrow assistant — a genuinely new and valuable skill. And the measurement should follow: track capability progression up the levels, not training completion, and track chosen commitment, not attendance. Those are the things that predict real adoption and the outcomes underneath it.

So the human arc of this stage is choice, then honesty about loss, then coached growth into a bigger version of your role. None of it is automatic or fast, and that's okay — we're not processing people through a change, we're helping colleagues we respect grow into work that's more human, not less. Next issue we reach the last stage of the playbook, Enforce, and what it really means to redesign the operating system so the change sticks.
